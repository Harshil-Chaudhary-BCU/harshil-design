01 / Context

The project started with a problem I had experienced firsthand.

Powerlifting centres on three lifts: the squat, bench press and deadlift. Its appeal is clear: measurable progress, growing strength and goals to work towards. But learning to lift and navigating setbacks can make that progress difficult to sustain.

When I started powerlifting, I was still learning the fundamentals. Poor posture and technique led to a lower-back injury, interrupting my training. Returning meant stepping back, following structured recovery exercises and gradually rebuilding my routine.

During my MSc UX project, a teammate shared a similar experience. That led me to ask: how could one digital experience help people learn, navigate recovery and return to consistent training?

Challenge

Explore the uncertainty people face while learning technique, managing setbacks and returning to training.

Opportunity

Investigate how guidance, training structure, progress and community could work together in one place.

Responsibilities

02 / Research

Understanding the people behind the lifts.

I wanted to understand what training and recovery looked like for the people behind the sport. Through surveys and interviews, I looked at what kept people from training, what interrupted their progress, where they sought support and which forms of guidance they actually found useful. The findings showed me where to look next.

To understand people’s training and recovery experiences, I conducted surveys and user interviews with my team. I focused the research on four main themes: goals, barriers, support and useful guidance.

Questions centred on:

  1. What are you working towards in powerlifting, and how do you plan your training and track progress?
  2. Can you describe a time when pain, injury or another challenge interrupted your training? How did you respond?
  3. When you’re unsure about technique or returning to training, where do you turn for support, and what remains unclear?
  4. Tell me about guidance you’ve used, such as videos, training plans or coaching. What made it useful or difficult to follow?

Competitor analysis

What existing products did well, and where they fell short.

I compared adjacent products to understand how they approached coaching, recovery, tracking and motivation. I looked for ideas that could inform LiftLab and gaps they left in a powerlifting journey.

Nike Run Club

Guided running, motivation and progress tracking.

Pros
  • Free guided plans: Made structured training easier to start.
  • Audio coaching: Offered guidance without constant screen attention.
  • Community challenges: Encouraged consistency.
  • Wearable pairing and shoe mileage tagging: Connected activity with training history.
Cons
  • Running-specific: Without strength-training or recovery workflows.
  • Limited plan depth: Less support beyond beginner programmes.
  • Reported sync and accuracy issues: Could undermine continuity and trust in progress data.

For LiftLabMake structured training approachable, with guidance that supports the task rather than competing for attention.

Annotated interface review

Prehab

Recovery, injury prevention and personalised rehabilitation.

Pros
  • Recovery focus: Prioritised recovery and injury prevention.
  • Guided content: A broad tutorial library and personalised plans.
  • Progressive-load principles: Provided a structured path back to performance.
  • Custom workout builder: Allowed flexible routines.
Cons
  • Paid access: Created a barrier for cost-sensitive users.
  • Limited progress tracking: Made improvement harder to see.
  • Reported reliability concerns: Accuracy concerns, bugs and video-loading issues could interrupt recovery guidance.

For LiftLabConnect clear recovery guidance with visible progress and a practical route back to training.

Annotated interface review

Strava

Activity analytics, live tracking and social fitness.

Pros
  • Analytics and live tracking: Made performance visible.
  • Device syncing: Reduced manual activity logging.
  • Community and challenges: Turned progress into a shared experience.
  • Fatigue and freshness tracking: Supported training-load planning.
Cons
  • Not lifting-focused: Core activity metrics did not explain technique, sets or recovery.
  • Access friction: Subscription barriers and desktop-only features added friction.
  • Privacy and tracking concerns: Could reduce confidence in sharing and interpreting data.

For LiftLabMake lifting progress meaningful, and use community to support motivation without replacing training guidance.

Emerging problem areas

Four problem areas emerged.

I compared the survey and interview findings with observations, secondary research and the competitor review. Affinity mapping revealed four recurring challenges:

Recovery uncertainty

Injury, joint pain and slow recovery made the next step unclear.

Technique guidance

Beginners wanted structured support without relying entirely on a trainer.

Consistency

Fatigue, disrupted routines and low motivation interrupted progress.

Fragmented support

Training, recovery, progress and community lived in separate places.

Defined problem

Powerlifters, especially beginners and those returning from injury, lacked a dedicated place that connected recovery guidance, technique, structured training and progress.

03 / Synthesis

One persona, shaped by the research.

I combined the recurring goals, behaviours and pain points from five research perspectives into one composite persona. Lisa represented the patterns shared across the research, rather than any one participant.

Composite persona

Lisa

Lisa is 23 Y.o and moved from Spain to London for a master’s in design at King’s College London. Her friends back home inspired her to try powerlifting. She liked having a goal to work towards and seeing her strength improve, but a back injury interrupted her training. Now she is trying to make sense of recovery advice and find appropriate support. She hopes to return to exercise gradually when she is ready, without feeling as though she has to figure out every step alone.

Needs

Lisa needs recovery information she can understand, appropriate support for making a plan, and a clearer path back to exercise when she is ready. As she returns, she wants technique guidance and a way to see her progress.

Pain points

The injury has interrupted the progress that motivated her. Advice is scattered across different sources, so she struggles to tell what is relevant to her situation. She also worries about doing too much too soon, while regular one-to-one coaching may be difficult to afford.

Goals

  • Understand her recovery options
  • Return to exercise gradually
  • Rebuild strength and confidence
  • See progress over time

Behaviours

  • Searches for recovery advice
  • Compares guidance from different sources
  • Prefers a structured plan
  • Tracks her training progress

Lisa combines recurring research patterns; she does not represent one person's exact experience.

User journey

The more Lisa searches, the harder it feels to begin again.

After her injury, Lisa wants to understand her recovery options and eventually return to lifting. She moves between video tutorials, recovery articles, community discussions and workout apps, hoping to find a plan that connects them. Each source answers a different part of her question, and the fragmented search gradually wears down her confidence.

04 / Ideation

Turning Lisa’s fragmented search into a connected product.

Lisa moved between videos, articles, community discussions and workout apps, but each source addressed only one part of her situation. Another isolated source of advice would not help. She needed recovery information, training guidance, visible progress and support to connect.

How might LiftLab connect the guidance Lisa was already searching for, without making her piece the journey together alone?

02 / Principles in practice

The principles became interface decisions.

I applied usability principles as I refined each screen. Every decision had a practical purpose: simplify the information, clarify progress or provide guidance when it was needed.

Minimalism

Reduce cognitive load

The focused home and recovery screens showed only what users needed for the immediate task, reducing visual overload while keeping progress and the next useful action clear.

System status + consistency

Keep users oriented

Timers and progress indicators made multi-step states visible, while familiar labels, icons and repeated controls kept movement between workout steps predictable.

Contextual help

Support users in context

Optional guidance remained available inside the exercise experience when needed, without cluttering or interrupting the main task.

03 / Refinement through feedback

Feedback improved both clarity and inclusion.

The community layout changed in response to feedback, while configurable colour preferences extended accessibility thinking across the wider interface.

Feedback-led refinement

The community became more distinctly LiftLab.

The first high-fidelity feed felt too similar to Instagram. I returned to an earlier direction and refined the feed so the community experience better matched LiftLab’s own product structure.

Accessibility choice

Colour preferences became configurable.

To support users with colour-vision differences, the interface offered primary and secondary colour choices they could distinguish more comfortably. The selected palette updated the wider app rather than one isolated screen.

03 / Reading the evidence

Useful evidence is also honest about its boundaries.

The result provides direction for another design cycle, but it does not validate every feature or prove any real-world health impact.

What the study supports

A testable, understandable concept

The core prototype journeys were usable enough to generate structured SUS responses and actionable feedback. That feedback also informed a traceable redesign of the community experience.

What it does not prove

Effectiveness beyond the prototype

Seven responses cannot establish statistical significance, formal comparative performance, clinical validity, sustained behaviour change or real-world recovery outcomes.

04 / Next steps

Next: test real user behaviour.

A stronger evaluation would expand the participant group, observe task performance directly and review recovery content with qualified subject-matter experts.

01Broaden the sample

Recruit beginners, experienced lifters and people returning from injury to compare needs across different levels of familiarity and confidence.

02Measure task performance

Record completion, errors, hesitation and time on task across the recovery, workout and community journeys.

03Validate recovery content

Review health-related language and guidance with appropriately qualified professionals before any real-world trial.

Evaluation takeawayI could not claim that LiftLab was effective in the real world. I could show that the concept was understandable, testable and ready for a more rigorous next round.
